In our rush to embrace the productivity benefits of Large Language Models (LLMs), we've overlooked a concerning side effect: the gradual erosion of our attention spans. While digital technologies have been chipping away at our capacity for sustained focus for decades, the introduction of LLMs represents a significant acceleration of this trend. These powerful AI tools deliver instant answers, generate content in seconds, and eliminate the "productive struggle" that has traditionally built our cognitive endurance.

Recent research from Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon University (Lee et al., 2025) found that for 40% of tasks, knowledge workers reported using no critical thinking whatsoever when using AI tools. This passive consumption pattern fundamentally alters how we engage with information and problem-solving. When we outsource thinking to LLMs, we're not just saving time—we're bypassing the mental processes that build and maintain our attention capabilities. As we increasingly rely on LLMs to handle complex tasks, our ability to sustain focus without technological assistance gradually atrophies.

## A Different Approach: Cross-Training Your Brain

Rather than focusing exclusively on developing work-related skills, consider a complementary approach: deliberately engaging in activities that strengthen your attention "muscles" outside the digital realm. Just as athletes cross-train to build overall fitness, we can engage in diverse activities that develop our capacity for sustained focus.

The key is selecting activities that:
1. Require sustained mental effort
2. Provide immediate feedback when attention lapses
3. Gradually increase in difficulty as your capacity grows
4. Engage different attention systems (focused, divided, sustained)

## Practical Implementation: The Slow Chess Movement

One idea is what I call the "Slow Chess Movement." Here's how it works:

1. Find a colleague who's willing to engage in a long-running chess game
2. Set up a physical board in a common area or use a digital platform that doesn't rush moves
3. Establish a simple rule: one move per day, with no other constraints
4. Once a day. Take time to deeply consider each move, exploring multiple possibilities
5. Discuss your thinking process occasionally to deepen the learning

This practice offers several benefits:

- **Sustained Thinking**: Unlike rapid-fire digital interactions, slow chess forces you to hold complex positions in mind over extended periods
- **Relationship Building**: The shared experience creates a different kind of connection with colleagues
- **Cognitive Contrast**: The deliberate pace provides a stark contrast to the instant gratification of LLM interactions
- **Transferable Skills**: The strategic thinking and pattern recognition developed through chess directly benefit professional problem-solving

The key to successfully countering attention decline is consistency rather than intensity. Start with just one activity from the list above, ideally something that interests you independently of its cognitive benefits. Begin with short sessions and gradually increase duration as your attention "muscles" strengthen.

Remember that attention span is highly trainable—research shows significant improvements with consistent practice. By deliberately engaging in activities that demand sustained focus, you can counteract the passive consumption patterns that LLMs and modern media encourage.
